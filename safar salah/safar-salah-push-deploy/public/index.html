      Features

      Built for the prayer you can't take at the gate

      Times that follow the plane

      The sun's position is recalculated every minute from the aircraft's estimated position, so a Maghrib that chases you westward shows up where it really happens.

      Qibla compass that beeps

      On the ground, the compass opens as soon as you switch modes and beeps and vibrates when your phone points to the Ka‘bah.

      Horizon dip at altitude

      From 11 km up the horizon sits about 3° lower, so the sun sets later than on the ground. Turn the adjustment on or off in settings.

      Six recognised methods

      Muslim World League, ISNA, Egypt, Umm al-Qura, Karachi and JAKIM, with Standard or Hanafi Asr.

      Three clocks at once

      Every prayer shows the time at your origin, at your destination and in UTC, so it matches your watch, your phone and the crew's schedule.

      How it works

      From route to prayer times

      STEP 1

      Draw the route

      A great-circle path is traced between the two airports, the shortest route over the globe.

      STEP 2

      Fly it minute by minute

      The aircraft's position is estimated for every minute, with a climb and descent at each end.

      STEP 3

      Track the sun

      At each point the sun's altitude and hour angle are computed and compared with the method's angles.

      STEP 4

      Mark each prayer

      When the sun crosses a threshold, that moment becomes a prayer time, with position, heading and Qibla.

      Questions

      Praying in the air

      Which time should I follow, my watch or the plane?

      Prayer times follow the sun where you are. On a plane that means the sun as seen from the aircraft's position, which is what this timeline shows. Your watch and the destination clock are listed so you can match the moment to a time you can read.

      Can I pray seated if there is no room to stand?

      Most scholars allow seated prayer on a plane when standing, bowing or prostrating is not possible, facing the Qibla as best you can at the start. Ask your local scholar for the ruling you follow.

      Can I shorten or combine prayers while travelling?

      Travellers are widely permitted to shorten four-rak‘ah prayers (qasr) and, in many schools, to combine Dhuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha (jam‘). The conditions differ between schools, so check the ruling that applies to you.

      Why is Maghrib later than at the airport?

      Two reasons. Flying west, you chase the sun. And from cruise altitude the horizon is lower, so the sun stays visible a few minutes longer. The altitude adjustment in settings accounts for the second effect.

      A prayer is missing from my flight. Why?

      Only prayers that begin between takeoff and landing are listed. On far-northern routes in summer the sun may never sink low enough for Fajr or Isha, so those times do not occur along the route; follow the guidance for high latitudes in your school.

      How accurate are these times?

      Treat them as good estimates. The route assumes a direct great-circle path and steady speed; real flights take detours and wait in holding patterns. A few minutes' margin before the end of a prayer window is sensible.
